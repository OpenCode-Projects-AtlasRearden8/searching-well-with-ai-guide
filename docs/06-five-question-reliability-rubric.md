# Chapter 6 — The Five-Question Reliability Rubric

*One page. Screenshot it, print it, pin it next to your monitor. Any answer —
from a chatbot, a search result, a friend's forwarded post, or this very manual —
faces the same five questions.*

## The five questions

**1. WHO says this?**
Is there a named author, organization, or source — or is it anonymous "studies show"?
Named and accountable beats anonymous every time.

**2. WHAT is the actual evidence?**
Is it a primary source (the study, the document, the dataset), or a retelling of a
retelling? One screenshot or stat with a real origin beats five blog posts repeating it.

**3. WHEN was this true?**
Does the content carry a date that matches the claim's shelf life (Chapter 5)?
Stale advice on moving targets is silently wrong.

**4. WHY are they telling me this?**
What's their incentive — inform you, sell you, outrage you, attract clicks, or
promote a product? Not all motives are bad, but the motive tells you what to
double-check. (And for what it's worth: we hold this manual to the same test —
our books and guides are sold elsewhere, and this site takes no affiliate links.
Still a motive; now you know it.)

**5. WHERE else does this same claim appear?**
Is it confirmed by an independent second source that isn't quoting the first one?
Two newsletters citing the same press release is ONE source with good handwriting.

## Scoring without shame

- **5 Yes answers** → safe to act on for everyday decisions.
- **4** → fine for low-stakes choices; verify the one weak question for anything that costs money, health, or reputation.
- **0–3** → keep looking. Not necessarily false — just not yet trustworthy.

The point isn't a score; it's that asking five questions takes less time than
undoing one bad decision.

## One-line versions (memorize these)

Who — What — When — Why — Where else.

The same five words work on a news story, a TikTok, an AI answer, and a car
dealership's claims. That's the whole skill, portable everywhere.
