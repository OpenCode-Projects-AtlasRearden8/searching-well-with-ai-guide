# Chapter 5 — Date Checks on Stale Advice

*The internet never forgets, and never throws anything away — including advice
that stopped being true years ago. A date check takes thirty seconds and saves
you from confidently following 2013.*

## Why this chapter might be the most important one

AI chatbots are trained on old data, and the web is full of guides that were right
when written and wrong now. If you don't ask *when*, you'll follow recipes like:

- Tax and filing rules from before the latest law changed
- Interface instructions written for software that's had three redesigns since
- Safety guidance that's since been revised (car seats, food storage, medication interactions)
- "Best of" listicles recommending products or tools that no longer exist
- Price and availability claims that expired the week after they were written

## How to date-check a page (30 seconds)

1. **Look for the visible date.** Article pages usually have one. But beware: some sites stamp a *new* date on old content. If the date is "updated" — updated is not the same as *verified*.
2. **Check the page's true history.** Use the Wayback Machine (web.archive.org): paste the URL and see snapshots by year. If the page you're reading is identical to its 2021 snapshot except for a new date stamp, it's old content with a fresh coat of paint.
3. **Search for the change, not the topic.** `did [rule/product] change 2026` or `[topic] new guidance 2026 site:officialdomain.gov`. This finds changelogs, press releases, and news coverage of updates.
4. **Confirm on the source of record.** For rules and policies, the original publisher — the agency, the company, the standards body — is the only date that counts.

## How to date-check an AI answer (15 seconds)

Add one line to any factual question:

> "Include the date or period your information is based on. If your training data
> may be out of date on this, say so explicitly."

You'll often get back an answer that says something like *"my information runs to mid-2024;
check the IRS site for anything after that."* That hesitation is exactly what you want —
an honest gap beats a smooth fabrication.

Then close the gap with a search (Chapter 1's hybrid move):

```
[the fact] 2026 site:official-domain.gov
```

## Freshness tiers: how current does it need to be?

| Topic type | Target freshness |
|---|---|
| Laws, taxes, benefits, visas, recalls | This year, ideally this month |
| Software tutorials, app interfaces | Within ~6 months (interfaces move fast) |
| Prices, availability, "best of" lists | Days to weeks |
| Science and health guidance | Latest consensus, not any single year — check for revised guidelines |
| Math, history, classic literature | Older is fine; the basics don't expire |

## Habits that make this automatic

- Set your browser's/search engine's default results to **"past year"**.
- When a chatbot gives advice about rules or prices, treat the answer as a *draft*
  to verify against this year's source — never a final answer.
- For anything safety-related (health, driving, children, food, electricity,
  structural), the date check is *not optional*. Outdated safety advice is where
  stale information stops being funny.
