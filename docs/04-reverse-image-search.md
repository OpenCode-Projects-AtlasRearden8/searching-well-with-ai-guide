# Chapter 4 — Reverse Image Search

*You see a photo: a "hurricane," a "hero nurse," a "shot of the new phone." Before
you believe it, ask the internet: where has this picture been before?*

## What reverse image search is

Normally you search *words* and get *images*. Reverse image search flips it: you
give it an **image**, and it finds every page where that same (or similar) image has
appeared — with dates. Ten seconds of work that unmasks most recycled, mislabeled,
and doctored photos.

## The free tools (in the order we'd try them)

| Tool | What it's best at |
|---|---|
| **Google Lens** (images.google.com or the camera icon in search) | All-around default; finds pages, products, and objects |
| **TinEye** (tineye.com) | Oldest-match hunting — sorts by *oldest first*, great for finding a photo's true origin |
| **Yandex Images** (yandex.com/images) | Often finds faces and crops other engines miss |
| **Bing Visual Search** | A second opinion when the others come up short |

They index different things, so checking two is meaningfully better than checking one.
All are free; none in this list pay us to mention them (no affiliate links — ever).

## How to do it (60 seconds)

1. **On desktop:** right-click the image → "Search image with Google Lens" (or copy the image address and paste it into images.google.com or tineye.com).
2. **On phone:** press-and-hold the image → copy it, or screenshot it, then use the Google app's Lens camera and pick the screenshot.
3. **Sort by oldest match** (TinEye's superpower). If a "March 2026 flood photo" also appeared on a page from 2019, it's recycled — not from this event.
4. **Check the surrounding context** of the earliest match: what did the original caption say? What event was it really from?

## What the results actually tell you

- **Many matches across many years** = a stock photo or a heavily recycled image. Not evidence of the current claim.
- **Earliest match is unrelated to the current claim** = mislabeled ("fake context"). Common with political and disaster images.
- **Earliest match is a product page** = likely advertising, or AI-generated imagery being sold somewhere.
- **No matches at all** = either very new, very obscure… or freshly AI-generated. No match is *not* proof of truth.
- **Only AI-generated-image sites match** = the picture was probably synthesized.

## When words beat pixels

If the image is from a recent event, pair reverse search with a text search for the
event's name plus words like `"photo" OR "fact check"`. Established fact-checking
outlets (Reuters Fact Check, AP Fact Check, Snopes and others) may have already done
this for you in the last day or two. Checking a rumor someone else already debunked
is the laziest — and best — form of verification.

## Beyond images

- **Video:** grab a distinctive frame (pause, screenshot) and reverse-search it. Works surprisingly well.
- **Faces:** engines vary in policy; Yandex is commonly best at name association. Only use for verifying public claims — never to identify private individuals, which in many places is both creepy and unlawful.
